What happens if you ignore a Toronto parking ticket?

Drivee Editorial Updated June 23, 2026 6 min read

Ignoring a Toronto parking ticket doesn't make it go away — but the consequences land in a specific order, on specific dates, and there's one window inside which doing nothing is actually fine. Here's the full timeline so you can decide what to do next.

In this guide

  1. Day 1 — the ticket itself
  2. Day 15 — the first late penalty
  3. Day 30 — the screening window starts to close
  4. Day 75 — escalation
  5. Licence-plate denial at the MTO
  6. Does it affect my credit?

Day 1 — the ticket itself

A Toronto parking ticket — formally a Penalty Notice under the Administrative Penalty System — is issued either on your windshield by a Parking Enforcement Officer, or mailed to the registered plate-holder for camera-captured violations (rush-hour no-stopping zones, school-zone enforcement, etc.). The penalty is the amount printed on the notice — typically $30 to $150 depending on the bylaw section.

As of Day 1, you have three options: pay it, dispute it, or do nothing. All three start a different clock.

Day 15 — the first late penalty

Day 15 is the first hard deadline. If you haven't paid and haven't filed for a screening review, the city adds an administrative late fee — usually around $25–$35 depending on the underlying penalty. A $50 ticket becomes ~$75–$85 in one day.

Critically, Day 15 is also the deadline to file a screening review without a late fee being added. Filing within 15 days pauses the clock. Late fees don't accrue while a screening is pending. So even if you intend to fight, file before Day 15 — not on Day 14 with no preparation, but file at minimum a placeholder request and supplement the evidence after.

Day 30 — the screening window starts to close

Between Day 15 and Day 75 you can still request a screening review, but the late fee added at Day 15 is permanent (it won't refund unless you win cancellation outright). Inside this window, the city's collections process is internal — no third parties, no credit reporting, no surprise.

Day 75 — escalation

Around the 75-day mark — the exact day can shift based on processing — the city moves the file out of the soft-collection bucket and toward enforcement. Two things happen:

  1. The screening-review option closes. After this point, you can only contest through a hearing review if you have very specific grounds, and the city's default position is no.
  2. The file is referred to licence-plate denial at the Ministry of Transportation Ontario.

Licence-plate denial at the MTO

Licence-plate denial is the real teeth in Toronto's enforcement. Once a ticket is sent to the MTO for denial:

  • You cannot renew your plate sticker until the full balance is paid — original penalty plus all late fees and administration fees.
  • You cannot transfer the plate to a new vehicle.
  • You cannot register a newly purchased vehicle under your name if you have an outstanding plate-denial flag.

Plate denial does not include towing your car, suspending your driver's licence, or garnishing wages. But functionally, if you can't renew your plate, you can't drive — Ontario requires a valid plate sticker for any vehicle on public roads, and the absence of one is itself a Highway Traffic Act offence.

Plate denial is forever

There's no statute of limitations on a plate-denial flag. A ticket from 2017 still blocks a 2026 sticker renewal. Some Toronto drivers discover ten-year-old tickets only when they try to register a new car. The fee schedule by then has stacked into the hundreds.

Does it affect my credit?

Toronto parking tickets are not reported to credit bureaus. The city handles collection internally through the plate-denial mechanism described above; it does not refer unpaid parking penalties to commercial collection agencies the way some U.S. cities do, and it does not file judgements against drivers.

The trade-off is that the plate-denial system is much harder to ignore than a credit-report hit. A bad credit score affects mortgages and loans. A plate-denial flag affects whether your car can legally be on the road tomorrow.

When waiting is actually fine

There's one specific case where doing nothing right away is harmless: if you've already filed a screening review and you're waiting for the decision. The clock stops while the review is pending. Late fees pause. The MTO referral pauses. You're under the city's protection — not in collections, not at risk of escalation — until the decision letter arrives.

Outside of that case, every day after Day 15 is a day you're paying for. If you're sure you'll pay the ticket and just haven't gotten around to it: pay today, not Friday.

What to do right now

If you have an unpaid Toronto parking ticket sitting in your glove compartment or your email inbox, the right move depends on the age:

  • Less than 15 days old — file a screening review today, even if you intend to pay. It locks the rate and gives you 4–6 weeks to think. Worst case you pay the original amount; best case it's cancelled. (Full dispute guide.)
  • 15–75 days old — pay or dispute, but don't wait. Late fees won't refund. If you have strong dispute grounds, file the screening review.
  • Over 75 days old — pay it before your next sticker renewal. The plate-denial flag is the real cost.
  • Years old — call the city's parking enforcement line (311) and ask for the current balance on the file. Sometimes long-old penalties can be settled in a lump sum without the full late-fee stack.
