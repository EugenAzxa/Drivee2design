Toronto parking signs explained — every colour, arrow, and time window

Drivee Editorial Updated June 23, 2026 7 min read

Toronto has three different "you can't park here" signs and they each mean something different. There are also permit signs, snow-route signs, rush-hour overrides, and arrow conventions that don't always match the painted curb. Here's a clear read of all of it, written so you can actually use it standing on a sidewalk.

The three "no" signs — they aren't synonyms

Toronto, like most Ontario municipalities, follows the Highway Traffic Act's three-tier prohibition. From least restrictive to most:

No Parking (white "P" with red slash)

You can stop. You can let passengers in or out. You can briefly load or unload goods. What you can't do is leave the vehicle unattended or stop for longer than is needed to do those things. If your engine's running and a driver is in the seat, you're generally fine.

No Standing (white "S" with red slash)

You can stop only long enough to immediately load or unload passengers. You can't wait for anyone. You can't sit and check your phone. As soon as the passenger door closes, you go.

No Stopping (white circle, red ring)

The strictest of the three. You may not stop the vehicle at all for any reason except a mechanical breakdown or to obey a traffic control device (red light, stop sign). Pulling over to drop a friend off is a violation. These signs typically appear in tow-away zones — the penalty is steep and removal is fast.

Quick rule of thumb

No Parking → you can stand and load. No Standing → you can stop but go immediately. No Stopping → you can't even touch the curb.

How to read the arrows

Every Toronto regulatory sign uses an arrow at the bottom of the panel to show the direction the restriction applies from the post:

  • Arrow pointing left only — the rule applies from this post going left, until the next sign.
  • Arrow pointing right only — the rule applies from this post going right, until the next sign.
  • Arrow with two heads (← →) — the rule applies in both directions until another sign cancels it.
  • No arrow — the rule applies only at the location of the post itself (rare).

The arrow direction often catches people out because they read the sign from inside their parked car and the geometry inverts. The arrow always speaks from the post outward — not from your perspective inside the vehicle.

Time windows and the "except" trick

Most Toronto signs aren't blanket bans. They're time-bounded — and the wording matters:

  • "No Parking 8 a.m.–6 p.m. Mon–Fri" — restriction is active during those hours only. Outside those hours, parking is allowed unless a different sign overrides it.
  • "No Parking 8 a.m.–6 p.m. except Saturdays, Sundays and public holidays" — the restriction is in force every day during those hours, except weekends and holidays.
  • "3-Hour Parking 8 a.m.–6 p.m." — during those hours, you can park for up to 3 consecutive hours. Outside those hours, the time limit doesn't apply.

The "except" qualifier is the source of more disputes than any other piece of Toronto signage. Read every line — restrictions are routinely qualified by holidays, weekends, snow days, or specific exception windows printed in smaller text below the main panel.

Permit zones

Toronto operates a residential on-street permit program under Toronto Municipal Code Chapter 925. Permit zones are signed with a white panel reading "Permit Parking Only" followed by a permit area code (for example Permit Area 5C) and the hours during which the permit requirement is in force — most commonly 12:01 a.m. to 10:00 a.m., or 12:01 a.m. to 7:00 a.m.

Outside those permit hours, anyone can park there for free unless another sign (e.g. street cleaning, rush-hour no-stopping) takes over. Inside permit hours, only vehicles displaying a valid permit for that specific area can park there. A permit for Area 5C does not get you legal parking in Area 5B even if the streets touch each other.

Temporary permits for visitors are available online at toronto.ca for around $9.27 per day (2026 rate). They're worth knowing about — they cost less than most parking tickets and they take five minutes to set up.

Rush-hour overrides

On many arterial streets — King, Queen, Bloor, Yonge, Dundas, Eglinton, Bathurst, Spadina — peak-hour curb lanes become moving lanes. The sign is usually a red "No Stopping" circle with a window like 7 a.m.–9 a.m. Mon–Fri (morning rush, downtown-bound side) or 3:30 p.m.–6:30 p.m. Mon–Fri (evening rush, outbound side).

Rush-hour zones are aggressively enforced and frequently tow. If you parked legally at 6:50 a.m. and didn't move by 7 a.m., the meter is no longer the controlling rule — the no-stopping window is. A 10-minute lapse can become a tow plus a ticket.

The 7 a.m. trap

Rush-hour windows start before most workdays begin. If you park late at night on a King Street block, you have to be gone before the morning rush window opens. Set a phone alarm — or use Drivee, which auto-detects rush-hour zones and warns you in advance.

Snow routes and winter

During declared snow emergencies, Toronto's Major Snow Removal Routes become no-parking corridors regardless of any other sign. The declaration is announced via the city's alert system, posted on toronto.ca, and pushed through major news channels. Once it's in effect, ploughs run continuously and any vehicle on a marked snow route can be ticketed and towed without further warning.

Snow-route signs themselves are blue with a snowflake symbol; they're posted year-round so you can identify a snow route any time. The temporary parking ban only activates during a declared emergency.

Curb paint vs. signs — when they disagree

Toronto sometimes paints curbs to mark bus stops, fire-hydrant zones, accessible-parking spaces, and loading zones. Curb paint generally reinforces what the sign says — but when they disagree, the rule is:

  • The sign controls. Painted curbs are auxiliary markers. If the paint says one thing and a properly displayed sign says another, the sign is the legal authority.
  • An absent or faded sign is not "the same as a painted curb." Toronto Municipal Code § 950-405A requires that signs be displayed. If the sign is gone, missing, or unreadable, the prohibition isn't validly posted regardless of what's painted on the curb. This is a recognised ground for dispute.

When two signs stack on the same pole

Stacked signs on Toronto streets read top-down by time period. The top sign is the rule that applies during the broadest or earliest time window; the lower signs are narrower exceptions or overrides during specific windows.

Example, reading top to bottom:

  1. "No Parking 7 a.m.–6 p.m. Mon–Fri ←→"
  2. "1-Hour Parking 9 a.m.–4 p.m. Mon–Fri ←→"
  3. "Permit Parking Only 12:01 a.m.–7 a.m. ←→"

Translated: at 10 a.m. on a Tuesday you can park for up to one hour (the 1-Hour Parking override beats the broader No Parking). At 5 a.m. on a Tuesday you can only park if you have a permit. At 8 p.m. on a Tuesday, no restriction applies and parking is free. The stack defines a single coherent schedule even though it looks like contradictions.

If you can't make a stacked sign make sense, take a photo and walk away. Genuine ambiguity is a disputable ground at screening review — but only if you have the photo.
