Visual reference · Toronto

Every Toronto parking sign — with a picture and what it actually means.

Toronto stacks parking restrictions on top of each other and most drivers have never been taught how to read them. Here's every common sign you'll see on a Toronto street, drawn at proper proportions, with a plain-English explanation.

Prohibitions — the "you can't" signs

Three different signs that all say "you can't" — but they mean very different things.

No Parking

The middle of the three "no" tiers. You can stop briefly to drop off, pick up, or load — but you cannot leave the vehicle unattended during the listed window.

Where: most residential side streets, near schools, near intersections, in front of fire hydrants.

Common mistake: running into a store "for one minute" — the second you walk away with the engine off, you're parked, not stopped.

No Standing

Stricter than No Parking. You can stop only long enough to immediately load or unload passengers. Waiting for someone — even with the engine on — is a violation.

Where: busy commercial blocks, in front of theatres and hotels, school drop-off zones during certain hours.

Common mistake: waiting in the car for a friend to come out. The door must open and close as the only event.

No Stopping

The strictest tier. You may not stop the vehicle at all, for any reason, except a mechanical breakdown or a traffic control device. These zones are usually tow-away.

Where: downtown arterial curb lanes during rush hour (King, Queen, Bloor, Yonge, Bathurst), bridges, expressway entrances.

Common mistake: parking legally at 6:55 a.m. and getting towed at 7:00 a.m. — once the no-stopping window opens, the previous rule doesn't matter.

Rush-Hour No Stopping (both peaks)

A No Stopping sign with two time windows — morning rush and evening rush. The curb lane becomes a moving lane during both windows.

Where: downtown-bound side of arterials in the morning, outbound side in the evening. Both sides of Bloor, Yonge, and King in the core.

Common mistake: reading only the top window and missing the second one. Read every line.

Permit and time-limited parking

These signs allow parking under specific conditions — read the conditions carefully.

Permit Parking Only

During the listed hours, only vehicles displaying a valid permit for the specific area code (e.g. 5C) can park here. Outside those hours, anyone can park unless another sign overrides.

Where: nearly every residential side street in the old city of Toronto, typically overnight windows like 12:01–7:00 a.m. or 12:01–10:00 a.m.

Common mistake: using an Area 5C permit on Area 5B streets. Permits are zone-specific — the streets can literally touch.

Full guide: permit parking explained →

1-Hour Parking

During the listed window, you can park for up to one consecutive hour. The "except by permit" line means permit-holders can stay longer.

Where: commercial strips where the city wants turnover — Bloor, College, Queen, Yonge in midtown.

Common mistake: moving the car two spots over to "reset" the hour. Enforcement officers chalk tires and use plate-recognition cameras — moving within the block usually doesn't reset.

Full guide: Toronto time limit parking →

3-Hour Parking (default citywide)

Toronto's default rule outside the downtown core is 3-hour parking. If no other sign restricts the block, you can park for up to 3 hours before being ticketed.

Where: almost everywhere in residential Toronto that isn't permit-only. This is the silent baseline most Torontonians don't know exists.

Common mistake: assuming "no sign = unlimited parking." Not true — the citywide 3-hour default still applies.

Street Cleaning No Parking

A weekly recurring No Parking window for street sweeping. Cars in the zone during the window are ticketed and often towed so the sweeper can pass.

Where: nearly every Toronto residential block, usually one morning per week April through November.

Common mistake: parking Monday night and forgetting it's Tuesday morning until the tow truck arrives. Set a recurring weekly phone alarm.

Paid parking and accessible parking

Pay-and-display zones and the blue permit signs.

Pay & Display (Green P street parking)

During the listed hours, parking is paid — buy a ticket at the meter or pay via the Green P app and display it on your dashboard. Outside the hours, parking is free unless another sign overrides.

Where: commercial streets across Toronto — Yonge, Queen, King, College, Bloor, Spadina. The green P logo is the give-away.

Common mistake: paying for one hour, going to dinner, and getting ticketed for going over. Pay for the full visit upfront — the app lets you extend remotely if you stay later.

Accessible Parking (APP required)

Reserved for vehicles displaying a valid Ontario Accessible Parking Permit (APP). Available 24/7 — no time window changes the requirement.

Where: reserved on-street spots near hospitals, civic buildings, and accessible commercial entrances; almost every public parking lot.

Common mistake: using a family member's APP while they aren't in the car. The permit-holder must be a passenger or driver — using it without them is a $450 ticket plus permit suspension.

Loading zones

Commercial loading and passenger loading are different things with different rules.

Commercial Loading Zone

Reserved for commercial vehicles actively loading or unloading goods. Under Toronto Municipal Code § 950-402A(1), "actively engaged" is the operative phrase — if no one is loading, the vehicle shouldn't be there.

Where: in front of stores, restaurants, and small offices in commercial strips.

Common mistake: parking a passenger car there for "just a minute." Passenger vehicles can't use commercial loading zones at all during the listed window.

Passenger Loading Only

Stop only long enough to load or unload passengers — typically 3 minutes maximum. The driver should stay with the vehicle.

Where: outside hotels, theatres, airport terminals, hospitals, and concert venues.

Common mistake: leaving the car to walk a passenger inside. The driver must remain at or near the vehicle.

Special zones — Fire, Snow, Bus, Tow

Signs that mean serious consequences if you ignore them.

Fire Route

Designated fire-truck access lane. Parking is prohibited at all times, no exceptions — including for the building's own tenants. Enforcement is immediate and tows are routine.

Where: driveways and frontages of apartment buildings, condos, hospitals, malls, and most commercial complexes.

Common mistake: assuming "I live here" or "I'm visiting unit 504" is a defence. It isn't — the route must stay clear regardless of who you are.

Snow Route

Posted year-round, but the parking ban only activates during declared snow emergencies. Once active, any vehicle on the route is ticketed and towed without further warning.

Where: roughly 200 km of arterial roads across the city — main routes the city needs cleared first when ploughs deploy.

Common mistake: ignoring the sign because it was fine yesterday. Sign up for Toronto's emergency alerts so you find out before the plough does.

Bus Stop (no parking zone)

No parking within 15 metres on either side of the post — that's about three car lengths. The zone is bigger than most drivers realize, so a "spot right behind the bus stop sign" usually isn't legal.

Where: every TTC bus and streetcar stop in the city. The pole is the centre of the zone, not the edge.

Common mistake: parking next to the bus shelter thinking the shelter marks the zone. The 15m measures from the post, which is sometimes 5-10 metres away from the shelter.

Tow-Away Zone

A reinforcement panel that accompanies other prohibition signs (No Stopping, Fire Route, Snow Route) to make clear that enforcement is by tow truck, not just ticket. Same rules — louder warning.

Where: below rush-hour No Stopping signs on arterials, below Fire Route signs at apartment buildings, in any zone where the city expects to use tow trucks first.

Common mistake: treating it as a separate, weaker rule than No Stopping. It's not weaker — it's the same rule with the warning that you'll lose your car, not just $150.

EV charging and school zones

Two newer sign categories worth knowing.

Electric Vehicle Charging Only

Reserved for EVs that are actively plugged into the charger. An EV parked there without charging — or any non-EV — can be ticketed.

Where: in dedicated bays at Green P lots, Loblaws, Costco, IKEA, and many condo visitor garages.

Common mistake: "ICE-ing" the spot — parking a gas car in an EV bay even briefly. Toronto's enforcement has been escalating fines on this.

School Zone — No Parking (school days)

During the school-day window, parking is prohibited within the zone — typically to keep sight-lines clear for children crossing and to let buses through. Fines double for violations in school zones.

Where: within roughly 150 metres of any Toronto District School Board, Toronto Catholic District School Board, or private-school frontage.

Common mistake: doing the school drop-off in a No Parking zone "just for 30 seconds." Camera enforcement is now common in school zones — a 30-second stop is enough to be ticketed automatically.

Common questions

The questions drivers ask most often about Toronto signage.

What is the difference between No Parking, No Standing and No Stopping?
They are three tiers of the same idea, not synonyms. No Parking is the mildest: you can stop briefly to drop off, pick up or load, but you cannot leave the vehicle unattended during the listed window. No Standing is stricter: you can stop only long enough to immediately load or unload passengers, and waiting for someone, even with the engine running, is a violation. No Stopping is the strictest: you may not stop at all for any reason except a mechanical breakdown or a traffic control device, and those zones are usually tow-away.
How long can I park on a Toronto street with no sign?
Toronto's default rule outside the downtown core is 3-hour parking. If no other sign restricts the block, you can park for up to 3 hours before being ticketed.
Can I park in a permit zone without a permit?
During the listed hours, only vehicles displaying a valid permit for that specific area code (for example 5C) can park there. Outside those hours anyone can park, unless another sign overrides.
How far from a bus stop do I have to park?
No parking within 15 metres on either side of the post, which is about three car lengths. The zone is bigger than most drivers realise, so the spot right behind the bus stop sign usually is not legal.
Can I park in a fire route if I live in the building?
No. A fire route is a designated fire-truck access lane. Parking is prohibited at all times with no exceptions, including for the building's own tenants. Enforcement is immediate and tows are routine.
Does a snow route sign mean I can never park there?
No. Snow route signs are posted year-round, but the parking ban only activates during declared snow emergencies. Once active, any vehicle on the route is ticketed and towed without further warning.
Can I use an EV charging spot if I drive an EV but am not charging?
No. Those spaces are reserved for EVs that are actively plugged into the charger. An EV parked there without charging, or any non-EV, can be ticketed.
